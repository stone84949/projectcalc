The Class A line. Under the federal rules a combination needs a Class A CDL when its gross combination weight rating (GCWR) or its actual combined weight, whichever is greater, is 26,001 lb or more and the trailer is rated or weighs more than 10,000 lb (49 CFR 383.5, 383.91). Both halves have to be true. A 14,000 lb truck pulling a 12,000 lb trailer rates 26,000 lb - one pound under. A truck rated 26,001 lb or more on its own needs a Class B, even towing a trailer of 10,000 lb or less.

What the GCWR is. FMCSA defines it as the greater of two numbers: the GCWR the truck's maker printed on its certification label, or the truck's GVWR plus the trailer's GVWR (49 CFR 390.5). A GCWR from a towing guide that isn't on the label doesn't count toward the first, and a low label GCWR never pulls the number under the sum. The CDL rules apply to driving a commercial motor vehicle in interstate or intrastate commerce (383.3).

How much can a hotshot scale? As much as every rating allows at once: the truck under its GVWR, the trailer under its GVWR, and the whole rig under the label GCWR when there is one. The truck payload is what's left for the gooseneck or hitch weight, driver, passengers and gear, so a heavy pin weight can put the truck over long before the trailer is full. Axle ratings, tire ratings and state legal limits still apply - check the scale ticket axle by axle.

10,001 lb and up. In interstate commerce a truck, or truck and trailer, rated or weighing 10,001 lb or more is a commercial motor vehicle under 49 CFR 390.5, CDL or not. That generally means a USDOT number, a DOT medical certificate (391.41) and hours-of-service limits (395.3), with exceptions that depend on the operation. States set their own rules for intrastate work.

About "de-rated" trailers. Some trailers are built and labelled by their maker at a lower GVWR, so that truck and trailer ratings add up to 26,000 lb or less. The rating that counts is the one on the original certification label. NHTSA's position, as described in an NTEA white paper (Oct 11, 2021), is that only the original or final-stage manufacturer or an alterer can assign a GVWR, and a new sticker on a used vehicle isn't recognized. And because the rule reads "rating or weight, whichever is greater," a rig loaded past its ratings can need a CDL anyway. Treat a lower rating as a limit on what you haul, not as paperwork - and check with FMCSA and your state before you buy around it.

Federal rules only, read from the eCFR on 2026-10-25. States can be stricter about license classes for heavy trailers (NATM, Jul 27, 2021), and hazmat or passenger work brings its own CDL rules. Common questions

Do I need a CDL for hotshot?

Only if the rig crosses the federal line: a combined rating (or weight) of 26,001 lb or more with a trailer over 10,000 lb is Class A. Non-CDL setups stay under that line by keeping the ratings low, which also caps the cargo. Put your own sticker numbers in above, then confirm with your state.

What is a 26K hotshot setup?

A truck and trailer whose GVWRs add up to 26,000 lb or less - for example a 14,000 lb truck with a 12,000 lb trailer. It stays under the Class A line by the ratings as long as the truck's door label doesn't show a higher GCWR and the rig is never loaded heavier than 26,000 lb; either one puts it over the line. The ratings also cap the cargo: in that example the trailer can't carry more than its own 12,000 lb rating minus its empty weight.

How much can a hotshot scale?

No more than every rating at once - truck GVWR, trailer GVWR and any label GCWR - plus the axle and state limits. The "most cargo the ratings allow" line above shows what's left after the empty weights; the hitch weight still has to fit in the truck's payload.

Can I de-rate my trailer to avoid a CDL?

Don't count on it. The federal test uses the manufacturer's rating, and actual weight counts if it's higher. A sticker change on a used trailer generally isn't recognized as a new GVWR. Ask FMCSA or your state before relying on any lower rating.

Does the 10,001 lb rule apply to me?

If the rig is rated or weighs 10,001 lb or more and crosses state lines for business, very likely: USDOT number, medical card and hours of service. Add your truck and trailer ratings: if they reach 10,001 lb, the rule is in play. Staying in one state puts you under your state's rules instead - check them.
