1. Know what a hotshot is (and isn't)

A hotshot runs a medium-duty truck - usually a dually pickup - and a gooseneck or bumper-pull trailer hauling for-hire freight, often loads the big Class 8 guys don't want: machinery, building materials, farm equipment, time-sensitive LTL. Under 26,001 lbs GVWR you can run non-CDL; at or above it, you need a CDL. The weight math changes your insurance, your equipment, and which loads you can legally take - know your numbers before you buy anything.

It isn't a shortcut to easy money. The operators who survive treat it like a business with six numbers attached to every decision. That's the rest of this guide.

2. Get your authority

To haul for hire across state lines you need operating authority from FMCSA: a USDOT number and motor carrier (MC) authority. The MC docket filing costs $300; the USDOT number itself is free. Expect the process - filing, BOC-3 process agent, unified registration - to take a few weeks. Don't pay a third-party filing service hundreds of dollars for paperwork you can do yourself on the FMCSA site.

While you wait: this is when you line up insurance, because your authority won't activate without it.

3. Insurance - the real gate

For-hire property carriers need a minimum of $750,000 in liability coverage for non-hazmat freight, plus cargo coverage most brokers require (commonly $100,000). New-authority hotshot quotes vary enormously - by state, truck, trailer value, and your record - so get real quotes from multiple carriers early instead of planning off somebody's YouTube number. This is usually the largest fixed cost of year one, and it's the number that decides your minimum rate per mile.

This is exactly why the load calculator asks for YOUR minimum $/mi: insurance, truck payment, and maintenance live in that number - the load only has to clear it.

4. Truck and trailer

Most start with a used 1-ton dually and a 30-40 ft gooseneck. Buy for the freight you'll actually chase: equipment haulers want low-profile decks and 14k-20k+ payload; lumber and general freight tolerates less. Have the rig inspected before you buy - a cheap truck with a $9,000 transmission bill isn't cheap. Put the payment and expected maintenance into your minimum $/mi before your first load, not after your first breakdown.

5. Factoring and getting paid

Brokers pay in 30-45 days; fuel stops don't. Factoring companies buy your invoices for a cut - commonly 2.5-3.5% for hotshots - and pay you the same day. It's not free money; it's a cost of the load, and it belongs in your math. Recourse vs non-recourse terms matter, along with contract length and whether they hold a reserve. Run every factored load through the calculator with the factoring percentage entered, so the cut is visible on every line.

6. Load boards and your first loads

DAT and Truckstop are the big boards; most new authorities start there. Your first weeks are about lanes and repetition, not home runs: learn which lanes pay, which brokers pay on time, and what your deadhead actually costs - the empty miles to pickup burn the same fuel as the loaded ones.

Build a simple rule: any load gets run through the panel before you accept. No exceptions, including "it's close to home."

Common questions

How much does it cost to start a hotshot business?

Down payment on a used truck and trailer, your insurance down payment, $300 MC authority filing, and a reserve for fuel and living costs while receivables catch up. The truck and insurance dominate everything else - price those two first for your situation, then set your minimum $/mi from them.

Do I need a CDL to hotshot?

Not if your combined GVWR stays under 26,001 lbs. Plenty of freight moves that way, but weight limits shrink your equipment and some brokers prefer CDL carriers. At or above 26,001 lbs, you need a CDL.

What should I charge per mile?

There's no single market rate that matters - there's your number. Compute your all-in cost per mile (fuel, insurance, payment, maintenance), add your margin, and that's your minimum. The calculator makes the load tell you whether it clears.

Is factoring worth it?

For most new authorities, yes - cash flow beats the 2.5-3.5% cut. Compare it against a line of credit and read the contract terms. Whatever you choose, the fee belongs in your load math, not ignored.
