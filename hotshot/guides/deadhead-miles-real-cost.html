Deadhead miles: what the empty miles really cost

The rate on the load board is quoted on loaded miles. Your truck burns fuel on every mile - loaded, empty, and the drive home. Deadhead is where good-looking loads go to die.

The trap

Here's a load that looks great: Houston to Tulsa, 540 miles at $2.85/mi - $1,539 gross. The board shows a strong rate. But the pickup is 80 miles out of position, and you know you'll burn fuel on those 80 miles with nothing paying for them.

That fuel is real money. At $6.28/gal (the U.S. average on-highway diesel price for the week of Oct 19, 2026, per EIA) and 8.4 MPG, every mile costs about $0.75 - deadhead included. The 80 empty miles cost you roughly $60 before the load pays a dollar.

The same load at three deadheads

Same load, same rate, same truck - only the deadhead changes. Watch the net per TOTAL mile (the number the panel stamps):

40 deadhead - 580 total mi$1.75/mi net

80 deadhead - 620 total mi$1.59/mi net

180 deadhead - 720 total mi$1.26/mi net

Each figure nets out fuel at $6.28/gal and 8.4 MPG, $45 tolls, and 3% factoring on the $1,539 gross. From 40 to 180 deadhead, the same $2.85 headline rate quietly loses almost fifty cents a mile. That's the difference between a load that clears a $1.58 floor and one that doesn't.

Loaded-miles math hides this completely. If your tracking divides by loaded miles only, your numbers are lying to you by exactly your deadhead ratio.

Rules of thumb that survive contact with brokers

Price the deadhead into the rate. If you're 80 miles out, the load needs to pay for 620 miles of fuel, not 540. Ask for it; the worst case is the broker says no and you've lost nothing.

Compare deadhead to the rate bump. A load paying $0.20/mi more but 100 miles further out is usually a worse deal. Run both through the panel and let the stamps argue.

Watch the round trip. If the destination has no freight, your deadhead home is part of this load's cost too. A lane that pays great one way and nothing back is half a lane.

Let the needle settle it

Deadhead is its own gauge on the panel, and the net-per-total-mile math runs automatically - fuel, tolls, factoring, all of it, counted over loaded plus empty miles. Set your minimum once and the stamp reads every load the honest way.

Common questions

How much deadhead is too much?

There's no fixed number - it depends on the rate. A $3.10/mi load tolerates more empty miles than a $2.10 load. Run it through the panel; the stamp is the answer for your floor, not somebody's rule of thumb.

Should I deadhead toward a better lane?

Sometimes repositioning empty beats hauling a load that pays less than the fuel. Compare both plans with the calculator: the cheap load that covers most of your repositioning fuel can beat pure deadhead - the math will show which.

Why does my per-mile number drop so much with deadhead?

Because the gross is fixed while the cost miles grow. 540 paying miles at $2.85 is $1,539 whether you drove 40 or 180 miles to get there - but your fuel bill differs by about $105. Net per total mile exposes it instantly.
